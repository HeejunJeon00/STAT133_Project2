Part 1

I explored the California Energy Commission’s dashboard on new zero-emission vehicle sales in California.

Here are three questions I was curious about:

  1. Which ZEV model had the most sales in California during the first half of 2026?
  2. Do some counties have unusually high sales shares for particular vehicle makes?
  3. Was there a rise in ZEV sales before the federal incentive ended in Q3 2025, followed by a drop in Q4?

For the rest of Part 4, I chose to investigate the first question.

The most commonly recorded ZEV model in 2026 year-to-date was the “Tesla Model Y”, with 53,682 vehicles. It was followed by the Tesla Model 3 with 17,827 vehicles and the Hyundai IONIQ 5 with 7,421 vehicles.

5. 2026 YTD ZEV Share

The dashboard reports a 17.5% YTD ZEV Share.

I cannot recreate this statistic from the County sheet alone because the County sheet contains ZEV sales but does not contain the total number of light-duty vehicle sales needed for the denominator.

The dashboard reports 865,303 total light-duty sales.

Using the dashboard’s numbers: 151,525 / 865,303 = 17.5% T herefore, the dashboard’s 17.5% value is consistent with the 2026 ZEV sales total, but I cannot independently calculate the denominator from the provided County data.

C. Market Concentration

I calculated the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) across makes for each year.

hhi_by_year <- zev |>
  group_by(`Data Year`, MAKE) |>
  summarize(
    sales = sum(`Number of Vehicles`, na.rm = TRUE)
  ) |>
  group_by(`Data Year`) |>
  mutate(
    share = sales / sum(sales)
  ) |>
  summarize(
    HHI = sum(share^2) * 10000

E. The Elon Effect

I compared Tesla’s share of ZEV sales in the Democratic-leaning and Republican-leaning counties provided in the assignment.

dem_counties <- c(
  "Alameda", "San Francisco", "Santa Clara", "Marin",
  "San Mateo", "Los Angeles", "Santa Cruz", "Humboldt"
)

rep_counties <- c(
  "Kern", "Shasta", "Modoc", "Lassen",
  "Tehama", "Kings", "Placer", "El Dorado"
)
elon_analysis <- zev |>
  filter(
    `Data Year` %in% c(2023, 2024, 2025),
    COUNTY %in% c(dem_counties, rep_counties)
  ) |>
  mutate(
    county_group = ifelse(
      COUNTY %in% dem_counties,
      "Democratic-leaning",
      "Republican-leaning"
    )
  ) |>
  group_by(`Data Year`, county_group) |>
  summarize(
    tesla_sales = sum(
      `Number of Vehicles`[MAKE == "Tesla"],
      na.rm = TRUE
    ),
    total_zev_sales = sum(
      `Number of Vehicles`,
      na.rm = TRUE
    )
  ) |>
  mutate(
    tesla_share = tesla_sales / total_zev_sales
  )

The strongest reason this analysis could be misleading is that the two groups of counties differ in many other ways, such as income, charging access, vehicle availability, and previous Tesla adoption. Tesla’s share was also already changing before 2025, so this analysis cannot establish that Elon Musk’s involvement with DOGE caused the difference.
